## 1. The three trimesters, as stat lines

### First trimester — weeks 1–13

```
BABY        poppy seed  →  peach          (~0.1mm → 75mm)
HEARTBEAT   from week 6, ~110–160 bpm
MISCARRIAGE RISK   ~10–20% early  →  ~2–3% after a heartbeat at 12 weeks
DANI        nausea, exhaustion, sore breasts
VISIBLE     nothing
YOUR JOB    absorb load, tell nobody
```

**What is actually happening.** Every major organ system is forming. This is the
most consequential trimester developmentally and the one where Dani looks
completely normal and feels worst. That gap is the whole story of the first
trimester.

**The exhaustion is not ordinary tiredness.** Building a placenta is
metabolically expensive — her resting metabolic rate rises measurably. She is
not being dramatic and she cannot push through it.

**What to do:** take over anything involving smell. Cooking, the bins, the
fridge. Do it without being asked and without announcing it.

### Second trimester — weeks 14–27

```
BABY        lemon  →  cabbage           (~85mm → ~370mm, ~1kg)
MOVEMENT    first felt ~16–22 weeks (first pregnancies: later end)
HEARING     from ~18 weeks — your voice carries better than hers
DANI        energy returns, appetite returns
VISIBLE     from ~20 weeks
YOUR JOB    logistics, scans, decisions
KEY DATES   anomaly scan 18–22w · OGTT 24–28w · Tdap from 27w
```

**The good trimester, and the one to use.** Nausea lifts, energy returns,
restrictions are still light. Travel, the nursery, the big decisions, the last
proper trip — all of it belongs here.

**Your voice carries.** Lower frequencies travel through amniotic fluid better
than higher ones, so from about 18 weeks the baby hears Nico more clearly than
Dani in some registers. Talking to the bump is not sentimental nonsense; there
is a mechanism.

### Third trimester — weeks 28–40

```
BABY        aubergine  →  pumpkin       (~1.1kg → ~3.4kg)
WEIGHT GAIN ~200g per week from week 30
DANI        breathless, broken sleep, heartburn, swelling
VISITS      fortnightly → weekly from ~36w
YOUR JOB    be reachable, be packed, be calm
KEY DATES   RSV 32–36w · GBS 35–37w · bag packed 35w · term 37w
```

**Term is 37 weeks, not 40.** The EDD is a midpoint, not a deadline — only
about 1 in 20 babies arrive on it. Anyone who says the baby is "late" at 39
weeks is wrong.

**Being reachable becomes a job.** From 36 weeks, the phone stays on and
charged, the car stays fuelled, and Nico knows two routes to Makati Med
including one that works in Friday-evening traffic.

## 3. Birth-partner playbook

This is the part that matters most, and the part most partners have never
practised. Read it before 36 weeks, not in the car.

### The 3 Rs — Relaxation, Rhythm, Ritual

Penny Simkin's framework, and the most useful thing in this guide.

**Relaxation.** In early labour, help her rest. Dim light, low voices, a
comfortable position. Coping well in early labour is what makes later labour
survivable.

**Rhythm.** In active labour, women naturally find a rhythm — rocking, swaying,
moaning on a pattern, tapping. **Whatever rhythm she finds, your job is to
protect it.** Move with her. Count with her. Do not interrupt it to offer water,
adjust a pillow, or ask how she is doing.

**Ritual.** Once a rhythm works, it becomes a ritual — the same movement, the
same counting, the same phrase, contraction after contraction. Repeat it
exactly. Boring is the goal. Novelty is the enemy.

**The signal that it is working:** she goes quiet and inward between
contractions. **The signal it has broken:** she looks panicked, catches your eye,
says she can't. That is the cue for the Take-Charge Routine below.

### Counter-pressure

For back labour, which is common when the baby is posterior.

1. She leans forward — over a ball, the bed, or you.
2. Find the sacrum: the flat bony plate at the base of the spine, above the tailbone.
3. Heel of the hand, or a tennis ball. **Steady, strong, constant pressure** through the whole contraction.
4. Ask once where it helps. Then stop asking and keep doing it.
5. It will be harder than expected. Brace with your legs, not your back — you may be doing this for hours.

### The double hip squeeze

The single most effective physical technique available to a birth partner.

1. She is on hands and knees, or leaning forward.
2. Place the heels of both hands on the **fleshy sides of her hips** — the upper outer glutes, not the bony crests.
3. Press **inward and slightly upward**, squeezing the hips toward each other.
4. Firm and continuous through the contraction. Release between.
5. Use your body weight. Your arms will fail before she stops wanting it.

This opens space in the pelvic outlet and relieves the pressure directly. Many
women describe it as the only thing that helped.

### The Take-Charge Routine

For when she panics, says she can't do it, or loses her rhythm — often at
transition, which is also when she is closest to the end.

1. **Get close.** Face to face, near enough that she cannot look past you.
2. **Anchor her.** A hand on her shoulder or holding her head steady.
3. **Make eye contact and hold it.** "Look at me. Look at my eyes."
4. **Take over the rhythm.** Breathe it with her, loudly and exaggeratedly, and make her match you. Do not ask — lead.
5. **Same words, every time.** "Breathe with me. One. Two. Three." Repeat.
6. **Between contractions, tell her the truth:** "That one is done. It's over. You did it. Rest now."
7. **Do not:** ask questions, offer choices, say "relax", or look worried.

**She may be rude to you.** She may tell you to stop, and then fall apart when
you do. Keep going unless she is consistently and clearly telling you to stop.
This is not the moment to take anything personally.

### What not to say, and what to say instead

| Don't | Do |
| --- | --- |
| "Relax" | "Breathe out with me" |
| "Calm down" | "I'm right here" |
| "You're nearly there" (you don't know) | "You're doing this" |
| "Does that help?" (repeatedly) | Watch her face and adjust |
| "It can't be that bad" | Nothing. Say nothing. |
| "Should I call someone?" | Call them, or don't. Don't make her decide. |

*Sources: Penny Simkin, "The Birth Partner" (the 3 Rs and Take-Charge Routine are her frameworks); DONA International birth doula training materials. Verified 2026-09-18. Confidence: high.*

## 4. At Makati Medical Center

**Confirm all of this directly — hospital policies change, and this is from
general knowledge, not a policy document. `confidence: low`.**

Questions to ask at pre-registration, ideally around 26–28 weeks:

- How many birth companions are allowed in the labour room? In theatre for a caesarean?
- Can Nico stay overnight? What does the room need to be for that?
- What is the visiting policy for the ward?
- Where does he park, and where does he go at 3am when the main entrance is shut?
- What does the hospital provide, and what must they bring?
- Is photography allowed? At what points?
- What happens if a caesarean becomes necessary — where does he wait, and when does he see the baby?
- How is the bill settled, and what does the HMO cover directly versus reimburse?
- Pre-register and pre-file the PhilHealth paperwork. Do not do this in labour.

**Practical Makati notes:** traffic on EDSA and Ayala can turn a 15-minute drive
into an hour. Know a route that avoids both. Keep small cash for parking. Have
the HMO card, PhilHealth number and a valid ID in the go-bag, not in a wallet
that might be left behind.

## 5. Paternity leave — how to actually get it

### What Nico is entitled to

| Source | Days | Condition |
| --- | --- | --- |
| RA 8187 | 7 | **Married to Dani and cohabiting.** First four deliveries. Paid by the employer. |
| RA 11210 allocation | up to 7 | Allocated from Dani's 105 days. **No marriage requirement.** Paid via SSS. |

**Read the condition on the first row.** RA 8187 paternity leave requires legal
marriage. If Nico and Dani are not married, those 7 days do not apply — but the
7 allocated days still do, because RA 11210 allows allocation to the child's
father or an alternate caregiver regardless of marital status.

Don't let the app promise 14 days without checking. Ask once in onboarding.

### The filing sequence

1. **Now (by ~week 27):** tell RCBC HR that a birth is expected, and ask in writing what they require and by when. Some employers want notice well in advance.
2. **Dani files** her SSS Maternity Notification.
3. **For the allocated days:** Dani completes the **SSS Allocation of Maternity Leave Credits** form. Both that and her Maternity Notification go to Nico's HR.
4. **Nico files** his RA 8187 paternity leave application with RCBC HR, with the marriage certificate if applicable.
5. **After the birth:** submit the birth certificate to close out both claims.

**Ask RCBC HR one more question:** whether the bank offers anything beyond the
statutory minimum. Many large Philippine employers, banks especially, do. It
costs nothing to ask and it is never advertised.

*Sources: [RA 8187](https://lawphil.net/statutes/repacts/ra1996/ra_8187_1996.html), [ParenTeam — claiming paternity leave](https://www.parenteam.com.ph/article/how-to-claim-paternity-leave-philippines). Verified 2026-09-18. Confidence: high on entitlements, medium on procedure.*

## 6. After the birth — what to watch for

### Dani's postpartum red flags

**Nico is the monitoring system for the first six weeks.** She will be exhausted,
sore, and inclined to downplay everything. Several of these can develop fast.

**Call or go in immediately for:**
- Bleeding soaking more than one pad an hour, or clots bigger than a golf ball
- Fever above 38°C
- **Severe headache or vision changes** — pre-eclampsia can appear *after* birth, and this is the one most people don't know
- Chest pain or shortness of breath
- Calf pain, swelling or redness on one side
- A red, hot, painful area on the breast with fever — mastitis
- Foul-smelling discharge
- A caesarean incision that is red, opening, or leaking
- **Any thought of harming herself or the baby**

### Postpartum depression versus the baby blues

**Baby blues:** tearfulness, mood swings, feeling overwhelmed. Affects the
majority. Starts days 3–5, **resolves by about two weeks**.

**Postpartum depression:** persists beyond two weeks, or starts later. Can begin
any time in the first year. Low mood most of the day, loss of interest,
hopelessness, guilt, inability to sleep even when the baby sleeps, no bond with
the baby, thoughts of harm.

**The two-week line is the thing to remember.** If it hasn't lifted by then, it
isn't the blues.

**Postpartum psychosis** — confusion, paranoia, hallucinations, grandiosity — is
rare and a **medical emergency**. Go in the same day.

### Paternal postnatal depression — Nico's own risk

**Roughly 8–10% of fathers experience depression in the first year.** The
strongest single predictor is the mother having it.

It presents differently in men and is therefore missed more often: irritability
and anger rather than sadness, withdrawal, working later than necessary,
drinking more, physical complaints, risk-taking, feeling like a spare part.

**The trap:** "she's the one who gave birth, I have no right to struggle." Being
depressed doesn't help her, and it isn't a competition. Getting help is part of
the job, not a retreat from it.

## 7. The practical skills

### Safe sleep — the ABCs

The AAP's rule, and the one that matters most:

```
A   ALONE     own sleep surface, nothing else in it
B   BACK      on the back, every sleep, until age 1
C   CRIB      firm flat surface, fitted sheet only
```

**Nothing else in the cot.** No bumpers, no pillows, no blankets, no toys, no
positioners, no wedges. A sleep sack instead of a blanket.

Room-sharing — the baby's own cot in the parents' room — is recommended for at
least the first six months and reduces risk substantially. **Bed-sharing is not
the same thing** and carries the opposite risk profile.

Other points: no overheating, no smoking around the baby, a dummy at sleep time
may reduce risk, and if the baby falls asleep on someone, move them to the cot.

**The 3am rule:** the danger isn't the plan, it's the exhausted improvisation.
Falling asleep on a sofa holding the baby is among the highest-risk situations
there is. If Nico is too tired to stay awake, the baby goes in the cot first.

*Source: AAP Safe Sleep policy statement (2022). Verified 2026-09-18. Confidence: high.*

### Paced bottle feeding

If Dani is breastfeeding and Nico gives a bottle, do it this way — it prevents
the flow-preference that makes babies reject the breast afterwards.

1. Baby **upright**, not lying back.
2. Bottle **horizontal**, not tipped up. The teat stays only half full.
3. Tickle the lips and let the baby *draw the teat in*. Don't push it in.
4. **Pause every 20–30 seconds.** Tip the bottle down, let them breathe.
5. Switch sides halfway, as breastfeeding would.
6. **Stop when they stop.** A finished bottle is not the goal.
7. A feed should take 15–20 minutes. Five minutes is too fast.

**Signs to stop:** splayed fingers, turning away, milk spilling, frowning.

### Swaddling

1. Blanket in a diamond, top corner folded down.
2. Baby on it, shoulders just below the fold.
3. One arm down at the side, wrap that side across, tuck under the opposite side.
4. Bottom corner up.
5. Other arm down, wrap across, tuck.
6. **Snug across the chest, loose at the hips** — two fingers should fit at the chest, and the legs must be free to bend up and out. A tight-hipped swaddle causes hip dysplasia.

**Stop swaddling the moment the baby shows any sign of rolling**, usually
around 2–3 months. A swaddled baby who rolls onto their front cannot roll back.

### The shift sleep planner

The single highest-leverage thing a couple can do in the first six weeks.
Fragmented sleep is worse than less sleep — one unbroken 4–5 hour block is worth
more than eight broken hours.

**Breastfeeding, with expressed milk available:**

```
21:00 – 02:00   DANI SLEEPS.  Nico does the wake, gives a bottle.
02:00 – 07:00   NICO SLEEPS.  Dani feeds.
```

**Breastfeeding, no bottles yet:**

```
Dani feeds — but does nothing else.
Nico does every nappy change, burp, resettle and transfer back to the cot.
She feeds and returns to sleep. That halves her waking time.
```

**Formula or mixed feeding:** alternate whole nights. One parent is fully off
duty and sleeps in another room. A parent who sleeps every other night properly
functions far better than two who are half-awake every night.

**The rules:** the off-duty parent sleeps somewhere else, with earplugs, and does
not get up "just to help" — that defeats the entire arrangement. Revisit weekly;
what works at week one will not work at week six.

## 8. Nico's own wellbeing

### Fitness — the off-season

Treat it as an off-season, not a collapse. The goal is maintenance, not
progress, and the season resumes.

- **20 minutes at home beats 90 minutes at a gym he won't get to.** Kettlebell, bands, bodyweight.
- Train when the baby naps, or at 5am. Fixed time, low ambition, high consistency.
- Sleep deprivation makes hard training counterproductive — it raises injury risk and blunts recovery. Go easier than feels right.
- Walking with the pram counts, and gets Dani a break at the same time.
- Expect to lose conditioning. It comes back quickly. Fighting for progress now costs more than it returns.

### Money — the four things to sort before the birth

**1. Life insurance.** The highest-value item, and the one most often deferred.
Term life is cheap and premiums rise with age, so now is cheaper than later.
Cover Nico *and* Dani — the cost of replacing unpaid care work is real, and
routinely underinsured. Rough guide: 10–12× annual income, or enough to clear
the mortgage and fund the child to adulthood. **Check what RCBC already provides
as an employee benefit before buying anything** — group cover may reduce what he
needs, though it usually ends when employment does.

**2. An education plan.** Philippine private education is the largest
foreseeable expense after housing, and inflates faster than general inflation.
Options: an educational plan from a pre-need company (check the provider's
trust-fund position carefully — the industry has a difficult history), a
straightforward investment account such as a UITF or index fund with an
18-year horizon, or a mix. **The earlier this starts, the less it costs
monthly.** Even a small amount from now compounds materially by 2045.

**3. A will.** The genuinely important part is not the money — it is
**naming a guardian**. Without one, the courts decide. Philippine succession
law also has forced-heirship rules (legitime) that constrain how an estate can
be distributed, so a Philippine will should be drawn by a Philippine lawyer.
A holographic will (entirely handwritten, dated and signed) is valid here but
easier to challenge. Also: update beneficiaries on SSS, Pag-IBIG, insurance and
any RCBC plan — those pass by beneficiary designation, not by will.

**4. The emergency fund.** Three to six months of expenses, in cash, accessible.
The new expense isn't nappies; it's that the household's risk tolerance has
changed.

Swipe cards. Affectionate, not preachy — everyone says at least one of these.

| "Is that allowed?" | Makes him the food police. | Learn the list yourself and stay quiet. |
| "My mum says…" | Two problems in three words. | "What do you want to do?" |

## 10. Lolas, titas, and pamahiin

Advice arrives constantly and mostly from love. The skill is deflecting without
wounding.

**The three moves:**

1. **Thank, don't debate.** "Thank you po, I'll remember that." No argument needed.
2. **Put it on the doctor.** "Dra. Villafria wants us to do it this way." Nobody argues with the OB. This is the single most useful sentence available.
3. **Nico takes the hit, not Dani.** If it's his family, he handles them. She is not obliged to manage his relatives while pregnant. This is the most important one.

**Where to actually hold the line:** anything touching safe sleep (no pillows or
bumpers in the cot, back sleeping), no honey before 12 months, no smoking near
the baby, no unsolicited kissing the newborn's face, and nobody visiting while
sick. On these, be warm and completely immovable.

**Where to let it go:** the safety pin, the belly-shape prediction, the food
arriving unannounced, the opinion about the name. None of it matters, and
accepting a harmless pamahiin graciously buys enormous goodwill for the times
you do have to say no.

**One genuine tension:** Filipino families visit newborns early and in numbers.
It is an expression of love and also an infection risk for a baby with no
vaccines yet. A workable compromise: delay visits for two weeks, then keep them
short, require handwashing, ban anyone with a cough, and nobody kisses the face.
Frame it as the paediatrician's rule, not as a boundary against them.
