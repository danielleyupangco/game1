Move

Get clearance from Dra. Villafria before starting any routine. This applies to every routine below and should appear as a persistent banner on the Move screen, not a one-time dismissal.

The general guidance is 150 minutes of moderate activity a week in an uncomplicated pregnancy, and that staying active is beneficial rather than risky. Walking is ideal in Manila — indoors, in a mall, when it is too hot.

The talk test: if Dani can hold a conversation, the intensity is right. If she cannot finish a sentence, ease off. This replaces heart-rate targets, which are unreliable in pregnancy.

The November walk

Dani is walking a marathon event in November 2026. Across that month she moves from week 11 to week 16, so the exact event date matters — early November is still the first trimester, late November is comfortably the second.

Ask Dra. Villafria first, specifically about the subchorionic hemorrhage found on 18 September. The evidence does not support routine activity restriction for a small SCH, but this is her call to make, not the app's.

Heat and hydration checklist:

  • Start before 6am. Manila heat by 9am is the real risk, not the distance.
  • 500ml of water in the two hours before; 150–250ml every 20 minutes during.
  • Electrolytes if walking beyond 60 minutes.
  • Light, loose, pale clothing. A cap.
  • A planned bail-out point at every kilometre. No prize is worth pushing through.
  • Stop for: dizziness, headache, no sweating despite the heat, cramping, contractions, any bleeding.
  • Walk with someone. Nico is the obvious candidate.
  • The talk test applies throughout.

Korea — 5–9 December 2026, week 16

The best-timed trip, and now the next one. Second trimester, past the nausea, well before any airline restriction, and a low-risk destination with excellent medical care.

  • No alcohol, including in the soju-heavy social context.
  • No raw seafood — skip the hoe (raw fish) and raw marinated crab entirely.
  • Icy footing is the genuine risk. December in Seoul is below freezing and pavements ice over. A fall in the second trimester is the thing to avoid. Flat boots with real grip, and no rushing.
  • Cold air plus dry indoor heating aggravates nasal congestion, which is already a pregnancy symptom. Saline spray helps.
  • Walking a lot is good; just keep the pace conversational.

India — January 2027, ~week 22

The trip needing the most preparation.

  • Book a travel-health consultation 4–6 weeks beforehand — so, by early December. This is the single most important item, because vaccine and antimalarial decisions in pregnancy need a clinician.
  • Malaria: risk varies enormously by region. Some antimalarials are contraindicated in pregnancy; others are not. This is a specialist decision based on the specific itinerary.
  • Zika and dengue: both present. Mosquito precautions throughout.
  • Food and water, strictly: bottled or boiled water only, no ice, no raw salads, no unpasteurised dairy (including lassi from street vendors), hot freshly-cooked food only. "Boil it, cook it, peel it, or forget it."
  • Air quality: Delhi and much of the north have severe winter particulate pollution in January. This is a real consideration in pregnancy. An N95 helps; choosing a different region helps more.
  • Confirm hospital access at the destination before departure.

Mexico — March 2027, ~week 30 — defer or cancel

This is the trip to reconsider. Not as a matter of caution, but because several separate factors compound.

  1. Zika. Mexico has ongoing transmission. CDC advises that pregnant women avoid travel to areas with Zika risk. This alone is sufficient reason.
  2. Week 30, and long-haul. Manila to Mexico is among the longest routings in the world — typically 20–30 hours with connections. At 30 weeks that is a serious DVT exposure and a long way from Dra. Villafria.
  3. Airline cutoffs are close. By the end of March she is at ~32w3d, inside the range where most carriers require medical clearance. If anything delays the return, the restrictions tighten fast.
  4. Distance from care. At 30+ weeks, preterm labour is a real possibility, and it would happen a very long way from her OB and her hospital.

If the trip goes ahead regardless:

  • Written OB clearance, and a fit-to-fly certificate dated within the carrier's validity window (often 7–10 days before travel).
  • Aisle seat. Walk every hour. Compression stockings. Hydrate continuously.
  • Travel insurance explicitly covering pregnancy, preterm birth and neonatal care abroad — confirm in writing, as most policies exclude all three.
  • Identify the nearest hospital with a NICU at the destination before departure.
  • Rigorous mosquito precautions for the entire stay.
  • The condoms-or-abstinence rule applies to Nico afterwards regardless.

SSS maternity benefit — and a figure not to trust

The law (RA 11210, in force since 2019):

ProvisionValueConfidence
Paid maternity leave, live birth105 dayshigh
Solo parent additional+15 days (120 total)high
Miscarriage / emergency termination60 dayshigh
Optional extension, unpaid+30 dayshigh
Days allocable to the father or an alternate caregiverup to 7high

The computation: Average Daily Salary Credit × 105, where ADSC is the sum of the six highest monthly salary credits in the 12 months before the semester of contingency, divided by 180. Qualifying requires at least three monthly contributions in that 12-month window.

The maximum is where reporting breaks down. Sources repeated "₱70,000 maximum" while also stating an ADSC of ₱1,166.67. Those are inconsistent:

  • ₱1,166.67 × 105 = ₱122,500, and ₱1,166.67 × 180 ÷ 6 = an MSC of ₱35,000.
  • ₱70,000 corresponds to an MSC ceiling of ₱20,000 — the old ceiling.
  • Independent 2026 contribution-table sources put the current MSC ceiling at ₱35,000 with a 15% contribution rate.

So ₱122,500 is the figure the arithmetic supports, and ₱70,000 appears to be stale text carried forward. The app should not display either as fact. Show the formula, let Dani enter her own salary credits, and link her to SSS to confirm — which is better advice anyway, since her actual benefit depends on her own contribution history, not the ceiling.

Sources: PCW RA 11210 FAQ (blocked; via search summary), SSS maternity benefit (blocked), Sprout Solutions 2026 contribution guide. Verified 2026-09-18. Confidence: high on the legal entitlements, low on any peso maximum.

Prayers

Four short prayers, one for each hinge of the day. The whole cycle takes under five minutes. It is meant to be sustainable at 3am in the third trimester, which is the only test that matters.

On waking — the morning offering

Lord, this day is yours, and so is the child you are forming in me.

I offer you the ordinary things — the tiredness, the work, the meals I may not manage to eat. Take the small discomforts and make them count for something.

Keep our cub safe today. Keep me steady. And when I forget to think of you, go on holding us anyway.

Amen.

At midday — the Angelus

Traditionally prayed at six, noon and six, and the most fitting of all prayers for a pregnant woman: it is the Incarnation, recounted three times a day.

V. The Angel of the Lord declared unto Mary, R. And she conceived of the Holy Spirit. Hail Mary…

V. Behold the handmaid of the Lord, R. Be it done unto me according to thy word. Hail Mary…

V. And the Word was made Flesh, R. And dwelt among us. Hail Mary…

V. Pray for us, O holy Mother of God, R. That we may be made worthy of the promises of Christ.

Let us pray. Pour forth, we beseech thee, O Lord, thy grace into our hearts; that we, to whom the Incarnation of Christ thy Son was made known by the message of an angel, may by his Passion and Cross be brought to the glory of his Resurrection. Through the same Christ our Lord. Amen.

Why this one. Mary was pregnant. Not symbolically — actually, bodily, for nine months, with all of it: the tiredness, the waiting, the not knowing. "The Word was made Flesh" happened in a womb. Dani is praying the Angelus from inside the same experience.

At evening — a short examen

Five questions. No writing required.

  1. Where was there kindness today — mine, or someone else's?
  2. What did my body ask for that I ignored?
  3. What frightened me?
  4. What am I grateful for, even if it is very small?
  5. Lord, what do we need tomorrow?

Thank you for today, and for carrying us both through it. Forgive me what I got wrong. I am too tired to list it; you know it all. Bless Nico. Bless our cub. Let us sleep. Amen.

In the night — for the hours when sleep will not come

Lord, it is late and I am awake again.

I will not ask for sleep, though I would like it. I will use this quiet instead: to be still with the one you are making, who is awake in here with me, turning.

Hold the both of us through the dark. Let the morning be kind.

Amen.

And when even that is too much, the oldest Marian prayer in the Church, from third-century Egypt — short enough for any hour:

We fly to thy protection, O holy Mother of God. Despise not our petitions in our necessities, but deliver us always from all dangers, O glorious and blessed Virgin. Amen.

— Sub Tuum Praesidium, c. 250 AD

kind: situational. These are the ones the app should surface contextually — the nausea prayer when she logs nausea, the scan prayer the morning of an appointment. All source: original unless noted.

Before a scan or a test

Lord, in a little while someone will look at our child and tell us what they see.

I want to hear that all is well. I am asking for that plainly, because you know I am asking for it anyway.

But if the news is not what we hoped, be in the room then too. Let us hear it together, and not fall apart, and know what to do next.

Whatever they find, this child is already yours and already loved.

Amen.

After hard news

I do not understand this, and I am not going to pretend that I do.

I am angry, and frightened, and I do not have anything to offer you right now except that I am still here and still talking to you.

Stay. That is all I am asking. Stay with us, and with this child, and help us put one foot in front of the other.

Amen.

When the nausea is relentless

Lord, I am so tired of feeling like this.

There is nothing noble in it and I am not going to pretend there is. It is just grim, and it is every day.

Get me to the evening. Then get me to tomorrow. And let this be, in some way I cannot see from in here, the price of something very good.

Amen.

When she is afraid

I am frightened, and I do not always know of what.

Of something going wrong. Of the birth. Of being a mother and not being good at it. Of how much I already love someone I have not met.

You knit this child together in me. You have not stopped paying attention. Take the fear — not away, necessarily, but take it from me, and carry it, because I have enough to carry.

Amen.

For Nico

Lord, bless the man who is going through this beside me.

He cannot feel any of it, and he worries in silence, and he is trying very hard at something no one taught him. Give him patience for the days I am not easy to live with. Give him the right words, or the sense to say nothing.

Make him the father this child needs. He will be.

Amen.

For the baby

Little one — cub, for now —

you are the size of something I could hold in one hand, and you have already changed everything.

Lord, guard the small unfinished work of this child. Every cell of it. Let the heart keep its rhythm and the bones grow strong and the mind be bright and the soul be yours from the first moment, which it already is.

And let us meet. Please, let us meet.

Amen.

In thanksgiving, on a good day

Today I felt well, and I noticed, and I wanted to say thank you before it passed.

Thank you for this body that is doing something extraordinary. For food that stayed down. For the kick I felt this afternoon. For Nico. For a due date to count towards.

Thank you. That is the whole prayer.

Amen.

Past the due date

The day came and went and nothing happened, and everyone is asking.

Lord, you know the hour. You have known it since before I did. Let me stop counting and let this child come when this child is ready.

Give me patience for the phone calls. Give me sleep. And soon — let it be soon.

Amen.

At the very start of labour

This is it. It is beginning.

Lord, I am frightened and I am ready, both at once.

Be in the room. Be in Nico's hands, and in Dra. Villafria's hands, and in every nurse's hands. Steady my breathing. Let me be brave, and if I cannot be brave, let me be carried.

Bring our child safely through. Bring me safely through.

Into your hands.

Amen.

The 3 Rs — Relaxation, Rhythm, Ritual

Penny Simkin's framework, and the most useful thing in this guide.

Relaxation. In early labour, help her rest. Dim light, low voices, a comfortable position. Coping well in early labour is what makes later labour survivable.

Rhythm. In active labour, women naturally find a rhythm — rocking, swaying, moaning on a pattern, tapping. Whatever rhythm she finds, your job is to protect it. Move with her. Count with her. Do not interrupt it to offer water, adjust a pillow, or ask how she is doing.

Ritual. Once a rhythm works, it becomes a ritual — the same movement, the same counting, the same phrase, contraction after contraction. Repeat it exactly. Boring is the goal. Novelty is the enemy.

The signal that it is working: she goes quiet and inward between contractions. The signal it has broken: she looks panicked, catches your eye, says she can't. That is the cue for the Take-Charge Routine below.

Counter-pressure

For back labour, which is common when the baby is posterior.

  1. She leans forward — over a ball, the bed, or you.
  2. Find the sacrum: the flat bony plate at the base of the spine, above the tailbone.
  3. Heel of the hand, or a tennis ball. Steady, strong, constant pressure through the whole contraction.
  4. Ask once where it helps. Then stop asking and keep doing it.
  5. It will be harder than expected. Brace with your legs, not your back — you may be doing this for hours.

The double hip squeeze

The single most effective physical technique available to a birth partner.

  1. She is on hands and knees, or leaning forward.
  2. Place the heels of both hands on the fleshy sides of her hips — the upper outer glutes, not the bony crests.
  3. Press inward and slightly upward, squeezing the hips toward each other.
  4. Firm and continuous through the contraction. Release between.
  5. Use your body weight. Your arms will fail before she stops wanting it.

This opens space in the pelvic outlet and relieves the pressure directly. Many women describe it as the only thing that helped.

The Take-Charge Routine

For when she panics, says she can't do it, or loses her rhythm — often at transition, which is also when she is closest to the end.

  1. Get close. Face to face, near enough that she cannot look past you.
  2. Anchor her. A hand on her shoulder or holding her head steady.
  3. Make eye contact and hold it. "Look at me. Look at my eyes."
  4. Take over the rhythm. Breathe it with her, loudly and exaggeratedly, and make her match you. Do not ask — lead.
  5. Same words, every time. "Breathe with me. One. Two. Three." Repeat.
  6. Between contractions, tell her the truth: "That one is done. It's over. You did it. Rest now."
  7. Do not: ask questions, offer choices, say "relax", or look worried.

She may be rude to you. She may tell you to stop, and then fall apart when you do. Keep going unless she is consistently and clearly telling you to stop. This is not the moment to take anything personally.

Dani's postpartum red flags

Nico is the monitoring system for the first six weeks. She will be exhausted, sore, and inclined to downplay everything. Several of these can develop fast.

Call or go in immediately for:

  • Bleeding soaking more than one pad an hour, or clots bigger than a golf ball
  • Fever above 38°C
  • Severe headache or vision changes — pre-eclampsia can appear after birth, and this is the one most people don't know
  • Chest pain or shortness of breath
  • Calf pain, swelling or redness on one side
  • A red, hot, painful area on the breast with fever — mastitis
  • Foul-smelling discharge
  • A caesarean incision that is red, opening, or leaking
  • Any thought of harming herself or the baby

Postpartum depression versus the baby blues

Baby blues: tearfulness, mood swings, feeling overwhelmed. Affects the majority. Starts days 3–5, resolves by about two weeks.

Postpartum depression: persists beyond two weeks, or starts later. Can begin any time in the first year. Low mood most of the day, loss of interest, hopelessness, guilt, inability to sleep even when the baby sleeps, no bond with the baby, thoughts of harm.

The two-week line is the thing to remember. If it hasn't lifted by then, it isn't the blues.

Postpartum psychosis — confusion, paranoia, hallucinations, grandiosity — is rare and a medical emergency. Go in the same day.

Paternal postnatal depression — Nico's own risk

Roughly 8–10% of fathers experience depression in the first year. The strongest single predictor is the mother having it.

It presents differently in men and is therefore missed more often: irritability and anger rather than sadness, withdrawal, working later than necessary, drinking more, physical complaints, risk-taking, feeling like a spare part.

The trap: "she's the one who gave birth, I have no right to struggle." Being depressed doesn't help her, and it isn't a competition. Getting help is part of the job, not a retreat from it.

Safe sleep — the ABCs

The AAP's rule, and the one that matters most:

A   ALONE     own sleep surface, nothing else in it
B   BACK      on the back, every sleep, until age 1
C   CRIB      firm flat surface, fitted sheet only

Nothing else in the cot. No bumpers, no pillows, no blankets, no toys, no positioners, no wedges. A sleep sack instead of a blanket.

Room-sharing — the baby's own cot in the parents' room — is recommended for at least the first six months and reduces risk substantially. Bed-sharing is not the same thing and carries the opposite risk profile.

Other points: no overheating, no smoking around the baby, a dummy at sleep time may reduce risk, and if the baby falls asleep on someone, move them to the cot.

The 3am rule: the danger isn't the plan, it's the exhausted improvisation. Falling asleep on a sofa holding the baby is among the highest-risk situations there is. If Nico is too tired to stay awake, the baby goes in the cot first.

Paced bottle feeding

If Dani is breastfeeding and Nico gives a bottle, do it this way — it prevents the flow-preference that makes babies reject the breast afterwards.

  1. Baby upright, not lying back.
  2. Bottle horizontal, not tipped up. The teat stays only half full.
  3. Tickle the lips and let the baby draw the teat in. Don't push it in.
  4. Pause every 20–30 seconds. Tip the bottle down, let them breathe.
  5. Switch sides halfway, as breastfeeding would.
  6. Stop when they stop. A finished bottle is not the goal.
  7. A feed should take 15–20 minutes. Five minutes is too fast.

Signs to stop: splayed fingers, turning away, milk spilling, frowning.

Swaddling

  1. Blanket in a diamond, top corner folded down.
  2. Baby on it, shoulders just below the fold.
  3. One arm down at the side, wrap that side across, tuck under the opposite side.
  4. Bottom corner up.
  5. Other arm down, wrap across, tuck.
  6. Snug across the chest, loose at the hips — two fingers should fit at the chest, and the legs must be free to bend up and out. A tight-hipped swaddle causes hip dysplasia.

Stop swaddling the moment the baby shows any sign of rolling, usually around 2–3 months. A swaddled baby who rolls onto their front cannot roll back.

Fitness — the off-season

Treat it as an off-season, not a collapse. The goal is maintenance, not progress, and the season resumes.

  • 20 minutes at home beats 90 minutes at a gym he won't get to. Kettlebell, bands, bodyweight.
  • Train when the baby naps, or at 5am. Fixed time, low ambition, high consistency.
  • Sleep deprivation makes hard training counterproductive — it raises injury risk and blunts recovery. Go easier than feels right.
  • Walking with the pram counts, and gets Dani a break at the same time.
  • Expect to lose conditioning. It comes back quickly. Fighting for progress now costs more than it returns.

1. Life insurance. The highest-value item, and the one most often deferred. Term life is cheap and premiums rise with age, so now is cheaper than later. Cover Nico and Dani — the cost of replacing unpaid care work is real, and routinely underinsured. Rough guide: 10–12× annual income, or enough to clear the mortgage and fund the child to adulthood. Check what RCBC already provides as an employee benefit before buying anything — group cover may reduce what he needs, though it usually ends when employment does.

2. An education plan. Philippine private education is the largest foreseeable expense after housing, and inflates faster than general inflation. Options: an educational plan from a pre-need company (check the provider's trust-fund position carefully — the industry has a difficult history), a straightforward investment account such as a UITF or index fund with an 18-year horizon, or a mix. The earlier this starts, the less it costs monthly. Even a small amount from now compounds materially by 2045.

3. A will. The genuinely important part is not the money — it is naming a guardian. Without one, the courts decide. Philippine succession law also has forced-heirship rules (legitime) that constrain how an estate can be distributed, so a Philippine will should be drawn by a Philippine lawyer. A holographic will (entirely handwritten, dated and signed) is valid here but easier to challenge. Also: update beneficiaries on SSS, Pag-IBIG, insurance and any RCBC plan — those pass by beneficiary designation, not by will.

4. The emergency fund. Three to six months of expenses, in cash, accessible. The new expense isn't nappies; it's that the household's risk tolerance has changed.

The subchorionic hemorrhage — read this part carefully

A subchorionic hemorrhage is a small pocket of blood between the membrane surrounding the pregnancy and the wall of the uterus. It is a common finding.

Hers is very small: 0.082 mL. For scale, that is under a tenth of a millilitre — a fraction of a drop. Size is the thing that matters most in the research, and small is the category you want to be in.

Being straight about what the evidence says, because it cuts both ways:

  • A first-trimester SCH is associated with a modestly increased risk of miscarriage — around a 1.9× odds ratio in a large 2024 singleton study.
  • But size drives it. Miscarriage was around 18.8% with large hematomas against 7.7% with small ones. The raised abruption risk attached specifically to large collections.
  • One honest counterweight: diagnosis before 7 weeks was itself associated with higher risk, and this was found at 5 weeks. So the early timing is a reason to take the follow-up scan seriously rather than to relax entirely.
  • Most small SCHs resolve on their own and the pregnancy continues normally.

On activity: the evidence does not support routine bed rest, and studies have not found that SCH diagnosed in early pregnancy changes delivery method or drives adverse outcomes across the board. Nausea — what actually helps

Ordered roughly by how well it works:

  1. Eat before getting out of bed — dry crackers, plain bread.
  2. Small and frequent beats large and spaced.
  3. Ginger: salabat, ginger chews, fresh ginger in food.
  4. Cold food often smells less than hot food, and smell is usually the trigger.
  5. Vitamin B6 (pyridoxine) — evidence-backed. Ask Dra. Villafria for the dose.
  6. Hydrate between meals rather than during.
  7. Keep a window open; Manila humidity in a closed room makes it worse.

When it stops being ordinary: unable to keep fluids down for 24 hours, weight loss, dark urine, dizziness on standing. That is hyperemesis territory and needs a call, not endurance.
