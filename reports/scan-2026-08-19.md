## Correction to Monday's report

Monday's scan stated the BSP policy rate was "around 4.25–4.5%." **That was wrong
— the rate is 4.75%.** The Monetary Board cut to 4.25% on 19 February 2026, then
**hiked 25bps to 4.75% on 18 June 2026**. I worked from a stale search result and
did not verify it against the rate decision record.

It changes the reading in your favour, slightly: the tightening cycle is already
underway rather than pending, and the increment still to come is smaller than
Monday implied.

## What moved

**Peso weakened to ₱61.785** at Wednesday's close, down 29 centavos on the day,
against ₱61.44 in Monday's report and in your ALAGA model.

**Electricity is in open crisis.** Meralco's rate reached **₱14.8261/kWh in July**,
up from ₱14.3496 in April, driven by a **70% hike in NGCP ancillary service
charges**, higher fuel costs from the Middle East conflict, and — notably — peso
weakness feeding directly into generation charges. Meralco is publishing about
this under the heading of an oil-driven energy crisis. The peso and the power bill
are now the same problem.

**Remittance growth hit a four-year low**: +1.7% year-on-year in June, $3.04bn.
The Middle East supplies over 17% of Philippine remittances, and the conflict is
disrupting them. Remittances fund household consumption rather than savings here,
so this feeds through to consumer spending with a lag.

**Goldman Sachs cut its 2026 growth forecast to 3.3%**, below Standard Chartered's
3.5%, citing inflation, weaker household balance sheets and moderating remittances,
and calling private consumption "subdued through 2026."

**Counter-signal worth holding:** BSP Governor Remolana said slow growth allows the
BSP to be *less* aggressive on tightening. That softens Monday's MUFG call for a
hike to 5.25% by year-end. The hike thesis is now contested rather than settled —
do not treat either version as fact.

### Recovery equipment — hold, pending a power-cost analysis

This is the finding of the week. **Electricity is not a side cost for cold plunge
and sauna equipment — it is the product's main operating cost**, and it just went
up sharply with no ceiling in sight.

A cold plunge chiller runs continuously to hold water at 3–8°C. A rough estimate at
July's Meralco rate: a ~750W chiller running around the clock consumes roughly
540 kWh a month, or about **₱8,000 per tub per month in power alone**. A studio
running four tubs plus a communal sauna could plausibly face ₱40,000–60,000 monthly
in electricity for the recovery equipment alone. *(Estimate — build the real number
from actual equipment specs before acting on it.)*

That cuts against the play from both directions. Your B2B buyers — the studios
whose expansion was the entire thesis on Monday — are absorbing a rising operating
cost that makes new equipment purchases easier to defer. And the home buyer,
already squeezed by inflation and softer remittances, now has to accept a permanent
addition to a power bill that is itself in the headlines.

**This is not a kill, it is a hold.** The demand signal from Monday is real and the
studios exist. But the unit economics of *owning* this equipment have shifted
against the buyer in a way the model has not accounted for, and the honest position
is to quantify that before committing capital.

**Do this before the play advances:** get actual power draw specs from two or three
manufacturers, compute the monthly running cost at ₱14.83/kWh, and ask one of the
operators on the Tatler list what their power bill has done this year. If a studio
tells you electricity is now their fastest-growing cost line, that is your answer.
